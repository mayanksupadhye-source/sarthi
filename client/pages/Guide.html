A little biology, with room for individuality

Four phases, many experiences

Hormone patterns are broad tendencies. They do not predict anyone's mood or tell you how she feels today.

01MenstrualBleeding · a new cycle begins＋

What’s happening: The uterine lining sheds. Estrogen and progesterone are relatively low at the start of bleeding. The first day of bleeding is cycle day one.

Possible experiences: Cramps, fatigue, headache, or lower energy can happen; experiences vary. Some people feel little disruption.

Care that may help: Ask before offering a warm pack, water, rest, comfortable clothes, or gentle movement. Don’t add pressure or assume she wants advice.

02FollicularFollicles develop · estrogen rises＋

What’s happening: Follicle-stimulating hormone supports follicle development in the ovary. As a follicle matures, estrogen generally rises and the uterine lining begins rebuilding. This phase starts on day one and overlaps menstruation biologically.

Possible experiences: Energy or motivation may change for some people, but there is no reliable emotional script.

Care that may help: Support routines or activities she enjoys without imposing a schedule or expectation.

03OvulationLH surge · an egg may be released＋

What’s happening: A rise in luteinizing hormone (LH) triggers release of an egg. Cervical mucus can become clearer or stretchier. Mild one-sided discomfort occurs for some people.

Possible experiences: Many notice no clear sign. Ovulation timing varies and the calendar cannot confirm it.

Care that may help: Respect what she says about comfort and intimacy. These calendar estimates are not contraception and should not guide pregnancy prevention.

04LutealAfter ovulation · before the next period＋

What’s happening: The follicle becomes the corpus luteum, which produces progesterone. If pregnancy does not occur, progesterone and estrogen fall before the next period.

Possible experiences: Some people notice breast tenderness, bloating, appetite changes, fatigue, or mood symptoms. Patterns vary between people and cycles; many have few or no symptoms.

Care that may help: Check in gently, listen, and take her experience as the source of truth. Don’t dismiss a feeling as “just hormones.”

Mood, PMS, and when support may help

Some people experience recurring physical or mood symptoms before a period; others experience little change. PMS is a pattern that can affect everyday life. PMDD is a more severe condition that can substantially interfere with daily life and needs professional assessment. This page cannot diagnose either. If symptoms are severe, persistent, or distressing, encourage a conversation with a qualified clinician; symptom records can help that conversation.

A practical little handbook

How to be supportive

Care works best when it follows her lead. These are openings for a conversation, not instructions about what she needs.

1When she feels physically uncomfortable

Offer a comfortable space, water, a warm compress, rest, or help with a practical task. Gentle movement is only helpful if she wants it.

“Would something warm or comfortable help?”
2When she feels overwhelmed

Listen without immediately correcting, fixing, or asking for a full explanation. Let silence be okay. Ask whether she wants company or room.

“Do you want me to listen, or would you prefer some space?”
3When frustration rises

Keep your voice calm and your words respectful. Don’t assume a disagreement is cycle-related, and don’t turn a hard moment into a diagnosis.

“You don’t have to explain everything right now. I’m here.”
4When she wants reassurance or space

Believe her answer and follow through. Reassurance can be simple; space can be caring too. Check back only in a way she welcomes.

“I’m here if you need me.”
5On a positive or energetic day

Enjoy the day and support her plans. A phase description never dictates what she should feel or what she should be up for.

“That sounds good. What would you like to do?”
6What to avoid

Don’t say “it’s just hormones,” insist on advice, force a conversation, or treat her differently without asking. Never use a predicted phase to overrule what she tells you.

Ask. Listen. Respect the answer.
